# Why Bread Has Holes In It

Cut open a slice of bread and you will see it is full of little holes. The holes are not a mistake. They are the reason bread is soft instead of hard like a cracker.

The holes are made by yeast. Yeast is a tiny living thing, too small to see without a microscope, and it comes as a dry powder in a packet. It looks dead. It is not. It is asleep.

When you mix yeast with warm water and flour, it wakes up and starts eating. What it eats is sugar, which is already in the flour. What it gives off is gas — the same gas you breathe out.

That gas has nowhere to go. The dough is stretchy, so instead of escaping, the gas pushes the dough outward and makes a bubble. Thousands of bubbles form all through the dough. This is why dough that sat for an hour is twice the size it was when you left it.

Then the dough goes in the oven. Two things happen. The gas inside each bubble gets hot, and hot gas takes up more room, so every bubble gets bigger. At the same time the dough around the bubbles firms up and stops stretching. The bubbles are now locked in place.

When you take the bread out and it cools, the gas leaks away. What is left is the space where the bubble used to be. That is a hole.

You can prove the yeast matters. Make the same dough with no yeast and bake it. You get a flat, hard sheet. That is what a cracker is. Same flour, same water, no yeast, no holes.

So the next time you look at a slice of bread, every hole you see was a bubble of gas made by something alive.
