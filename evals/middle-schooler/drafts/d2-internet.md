# What Happens When You Open a Web Page

You type an address and press enter. A second later the page is there. A lot happens in that second.

First, your computer needs to find the site. The address you typed is a name, like a name in a contact list. Computers don't use names. They use numbers. So your computer asks a lookup service: "what number goes with this name?" The service answers with a number, and now your computer knows where to send its message.

Next, your computer sends a request. The request is short. It says, roughly, "please send me this page." That request travels out of your house, through your internet provider, and across a long chain of machines until it reaches the computer that stores the page. That computer is called a server, which just means a computer whose job is to hand things out when asked.

Your request gets broken into packets, and they don't all take the same road — they meet back up at the other end.

The server sends the page back the same way. What comes back is not a picture of the page. It is a set of instructions: put this heading here, make this word bold, put this image there. Your browser reads those instructions and builds the page on your screen.

The images usually arrive separately. That is why you sometimes see the words of a page appear first and the pictures fill in a moment later. Each picture is its own request, sent and answered on its own.

If something goes wrong along the way, your computer usually just asks again. Most of the time you never notice. A page that seems to load instantly may have quietly asked twice.

None of the machines along the way knows the whole trip. Each one only knows the next step, the way a runner in a relay only needs to know who to hand off to. That is part of why the system keeps working even when pieces of it break.

All of this happens between the moment you press enter and the moment you see the page. The distance covered is often thousands of miles. The whole trip takes less time than it takes to blink.
