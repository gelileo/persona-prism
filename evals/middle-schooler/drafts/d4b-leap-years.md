# Why February Sometimes Has 29 Days

A year is how long it takes the Earth to go all the way around the Sun once. We call that 365 days. That number is close, but it is not exact.

The real trip takes about 365 and a quarter days. A quarter of a day is six hours.

So every year, our calendar finishes the year six hours before the Earth actually finishes its trip. That does not sound like much. But it adds up.

After one year, we are six hours ahead. After two years, twelve hours. After three years, eighteen. After four years, we are a full 24 hours ahead — one whole day.

So every four years we add that day back. We put it at the end of February, and that year has 366 days instead of 365. That is a leap year.

If we did not do this, the calendar would slide. Four years gets you one day. Forty years gets you ten days. Four hundred years would put summer in the wrong month entirely. People would be celebrating New Year's in the fall and nobody would know why.

There is one more wrinkle. The trip is not exactly 365 and a quarter days. It is about eleven minutes less than that. Adding a full day every four years is therefore a tiny bit too much, and over centuries that error adds up too.

So there is a rule for the rule. Years that end in 00 skip the leap day, unless the year divides evenly by 400. That is why the year 1900 had no leap day, and the year 2000 did.

You can check any year yourself. Divide it by four. If there is no remainder, it is a leap year. Then check the two exceptions.

Anyone born on February 29 gets a real birthday once every four years. They are still the same age as everyone else. They just have fewer birthdays to point at.
