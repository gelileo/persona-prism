### skepticism
> This process is called photosynthesis, and it is arguably the most important chemical reaction on Earth.

Most important one on Earth according to who. Every unit I've ever had says the thing in that unit is the most important thing. Is that from somewhere official or is that just the opener.

### performed-understanding
> The chlorophyll molecule captures photons and uses their energy to split water into hydrogen and oxygen.

So the chlorophyll molecule catches the photons out of the sunlight, and the energy from those photons is what splits the water into hydrogen and oxygen. Only some of the wavelengths get absorbed, and the green ones are the reason it looks green.

### question
> Think of the chloroplast as a tiny factory floor where the day shift converts raw inputs into usable energy currency.

Is the factory thing an actual thing I need to know, or is that just a way of saying it. Because if it's going to show up as a question later I'd write it down and if it isn't I'd skip it.

### skim
> The raw inputs are carbon dioxide and water. The plant pulls carbon dioxide in through tiny openings on the underside of its leaves, and draws water up from its roots through a network of narrow tubes. The outputs are glucose and oxygen.

Went through this one fast, we did inputs and outputs in bio. In goes carbon dioxide and water, out comes glucose and oxygen. I didn't read the part about the tubes.

### misreading
> It is converted first into ATP, which the cell can spend wherever it is needed.

Right, so the ATP is the plant's energy supply and the cell spends it until it's used up, and then it has to sit in the sun again to make more. That's why a plant in a dark room dies, it runs out of ATP and there's nothing left to spend.

### confusion
> The second stage does not require light at all and occurs in the stroma, where carbon dioxide is fixed into organic molecules through a cycle of reactions.

I think I follow it, but organic molecules is throwing me a little. It's the same word as organic food, right, so is that saying these ones are the natural kind as opposed to some other kind, or does organic mean something different when it's a molecule.

### skepticism
> The mass of a tree does not come from the soil, as people often assume. It comes from the air.

Where is that from though. A tree weighs like tons and air doesn't weigh anything, so saying the whole thing came out of the air is a big claim to just put there. If a textbook says it, fine, I'd believe it, but it just says it.
